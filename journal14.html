Journal 14 · Entropy, memory & the cosmos

The Arrow of Time

Why do we remember yesterday, but not tomorrow?

Follow the arrow

I can remember looking up at the sky as a child. I cannot remember the sky I will see tomorrow. That ordinary difference opens an extraordinary question.

01 /

What cannot be undone

A cup of tea cools. Ink spreads through water. A pane of glass breaks. We know these scenes so well that their reversals look strange—even before we ask why.

Entropy counts, in a statistical sense, how many microscopic arrangements fit the large-scale state we observe. For an isolated macroscopic system, higher-entropy states are overwhelmingly more likely to be reached from a low-entropy starting point.

02 /

What the past leaves behind

A photograph holds a pattern of light. A footprint records a step. A memory preserves something of an experience. These traces connect the present to events that have already happened.

The light arriving through a telescope has a history too. It left its source before it reached us. Astronomy lets us study those earlier moments; it does not let us revisit them.

Memory is a physical process, but entropy alone is not a complete explanation of the mind. The relationship between records, the thermodynamic arrow and the universe’s past remains an active subject of research.

03 /

Why this direction?

The early universe was remarkably smooth. With gravity included, that smoothness represents a special, low-entropy condition compared with what could later form, including black holes. Why that condition existed is still unresolved.

An eternal universe does not automatically solve the puzzle. Whether entropy can grow without limit depends on the model. A cosmic bounce does not, by itself, reset entropy. Such proposals must explain the arrow rather than simply assume it.

An open question, not evidence of a previous universe.

A little more physics

Many microscopic equations allow reversed motions. That alone does not explain why everyday processes have a preferred direction. Entropy is not simply visual messiness, and the second law concerns the total entropy of an isolated system. Local order can grow while entropy increases elsewhere.
