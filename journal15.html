Journal 15 · Black holes & information

The Universe on the Surface

How much can a boundary tell us about everything within it?

Explore the boundary

I tend to imagine that understanding something means looking inside it. A shell conceals an interior. A door opens into a room. But black holes suggest a different place to look: the boundary itself.

01 /

The measure of darkness

An event horizon is not a solid shell. It is a boundary in spacetime beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers. Yet this invisible boundary has a measurable area—and a remarkable connection to entropy.

The Bekenstein–Hawking formula assigns a black hole entropy proportional to its horizon area. Double the radius of a non-rotating, uncharged black hole, and that area quadruples. Its entropy does too. The relationship follows the surface, not a simple measure of interior volume.

Another description of reality

This helped inspire the holographic principle: the possibility that physics within a region can be described using degrees of freedom on a lower-dimensional boundary. It challenges the familiar idea that more space must always mean proportionally more independent information.

In a leading theoretical example, AdS/CFT, a gravitational description is related to a quantum description on its boundary. This is not a claim that reality is a projected movie. It is a proposed equivalence between descriptions, supported by extensive calculations in particular settings. Those settings are not our observed universe.

03 /

What makes space possible?

Perhaps space is not simply the container in which everything happens. Some research explores whether its geometry can arise from quantum relationships. This is a serious possibility to investigate, not an established answer about the cosmos we inhabit.

For me, the fascination lies in that change of perspective. A boundary that seems to hide everything may also offer a clue. Looking into the darkness, we may need to ask not only what is inside, but what the edge can teach us.

Hawking radiation raises the information puzzle: how is quantum information preserved as a black hole evaporates? Holographic calculations support preservation in certain models, but a full account for realistic black holes is still being developed. The horizon is not a physical hard drive, and holography does not show that our universe is a simulation.
