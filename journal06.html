01 / The Night Before Tomorrow

Everything moves.
Even the silence.

The night was unusually quiet. Outside the window, a thick golden fog swallowed the streets.

Streetlights glowed like distant stars suspended in an unfamiliar atmosphere. Beyond the glass, everything seemed motionless.

Yet nothing was truly standing still. The Earth was rotating. Our planet was orbiting the Sun. The Sun was travelling through the Milky Way, carrying the entire Solar System through space.

And light was moving through it all — at approximately 300,000 kilometres every second.

Every reflection in the window, every distant streetlight, every object emerging from the fog was sending information from a moment that had already passed.

The present, it seemed, was always arriving a little late.

A moment suspended in light
02 / The City of Light

The night sky
is an archive.

Somewhere along the North Sea coast, the night was alive with artificial stars.

A giant illuminated wheel turned above the water. Buildings stretched over the waves, their lights scattering across the wet sand.

Every illuminated window was a message travelling through space. Every reflection carried a record of something that had already happened.

We never see the universe exactly as it is. We see it as its light reaches us.

03 / Back to the Future

What if we could
outrun tomorrow?

Imagine a spacecraft waiting beyond the atmosphere. Its engines awaken.

The Earth becomes smaller. The oceans turn into blue patterns beneath drifting clouds. The Sun grows into a brilliant point against an endless black sky.

Ten percent of light speed. Fifty percent. Ninety percent. Ninety-nine percent.

According to Einstein's special relativity, a traveller moving at 99.9% of the speed of light would experience roughly one year for every 22.4 years elapsed in Earth's frame, during a constant-speed journey.

Not through magic. Not through a mysterious portal. But through the geometry of spacetime.

The future is a destination we can move toward at different rates. But there is a price: we cannot return to the world exactly as we left it.

Motion / Time / Light
04 / The Last Sunset

A photograph is
a time machine.

The journey ends where it began. On the shore.

The Sun hangs low above the North Sea, painting the horizon in amber and gold. Waves approach the beach, carrying reflections that break apart and disappear.

A solitary figure stands before the ocean, holding a camera toward the light. Perhaps trying to capture a beautiful sunset. Perhaps trying to preserve something that cannot be preserved.

A moment.

By the time the photograph is taken, the sunlight it records has already travelled millions of kilometres. Tomorrow, the image will show that moment again.

A photograph is a small window into the past. A telescope is a much larger one.

05 / The Observer

Somewhere
in between.

We remember yesterday. We experience today. We imagine tomorrow.

Yet the universe does not provide a single universal clock shared by every observer. Time depends on motion and gravity.

Somewhere, light from Earth is travelling outward into the cosmos. The light reflected from our oceans, our cities, and our faces continues its journey, growing fainter as it spreads.

In principle, an observer far enough away receiving that old light would see an earlier chapter of our history.

Not our future. Our past.

To look far enough into space is to look backward in time. And to travel fast enough through space is to move forward through time at a different rate.
